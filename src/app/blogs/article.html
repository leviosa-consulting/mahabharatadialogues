What do we want? Culture!

Culture is a word that sits comfortably in posters, slide decks, and annual reports. But when you ask teams what it feels like, you often get a pause. Everyone believes in it, but few can describe how to live it every day.

That’s where Culture Architects began. It began as an attempt to take culture off the wall and place it squarely on the table.

The Challenge

The Tax business of a large professional services firm wanted to bring its culture to life in a way that was not just talked about but experienced. They had seven “culture contours” — Inclusiveness, Communication, Empowerment, Strategic Intent, Ownership, Trustworthiness, and Well-being — that expressed how the team aspired to think, act, and interact.

The challenge was to make these contours real for the 6000+ people who made up the business. Leaders wanted a way for people to see these values in action, to feel them in the spaces where they worked — from the lobby to the cubicle to the cafeteria.

And so the idea emerged: instead of another town hall or training, what if teams could build their culture together?

The Idea

We designed Culture Architects as a hands-on workshop and game that turned participants into designers of their own workplace culture. Each team was tasked with building an “office” that reflected the seven culture contours.

But this wasn’t just a metaphorical exercise. Teams physically ran around collecting cards that represented different elements of a workplace — a welcome mat, a round table, a plant wall, a strategy board, a wellness corner — and traded them with other teams to complete their space.

Every trade, negotiation, and choice revealed something about the real culture of the team: who takes ownership, who collaborates, who hoards, who trusts, and who empowers.

The Method

The journey unfolded in five acts:

  1. Understanding Culture —

    Participants began by exploring what culture really means: how we think, act, and interact every day. They shared what they enjoyed about working in their teams and how those behaviours shaped the culture around them.

  2. Discovering the Seven Contours —

    Through a lively “Culture Bingo” icebreaker, participants shared experiences that quietly revealed the contours in action. The room buzzed with laughter, stories, and quick recognitions of “That’s so us!”

  3. Becoming Culture Architects —

    Teams then played the Culture Architects game. Each group took on a space — the Lobby, Conference Room, Cubicle, Cafeteria, Townhall, or Stakeholder Station — and designed it to represent the culture they wanted. They traded cards, negotiated with neighbours, and discovered how inclusion, communication, empowerment, and ownership all collided in action.

  4. Building the Office —

    Teams then selected five final elements to design their ideal workspace. Every choice sparked debate. Was a “supervised desk” a symbol of transparency or mistrust? Did a “welcome mat” mean inclusion, or was it too symbolic? These conversations mirrored real workplace dilemmas.

  5. Commitment to Culture —

    Finally, participants created their personal “Team Contracts,” writing down one behaviour they would keep (“In”) and one they would drop (“Out”). From micromanaging to active listening, from “talking over” to “talking with,” the list was real, honest, and practical.

The Impact

Over several months, more than 100 Culture Champions were trained to facilitate Culture Architects across teams and locations. The program cascaded organically — champion to team, team to department, department to the whole business.

Culture stopped being a set of abstract words. It became visible. You could see it in how meetings began, how recognition happened, how feedback was given, and how teams cared for each other’s well-being.

Leaders began using the seven contours as a shared language. New joiners spoke about “ownership” and “trustworthiness” in their induction sessions. Even the office spaces began to change — not because someone mandated it, but because people started designing their workday differently.

What We Learned

Culture cannot be delivered. It has to be designed together.

And when people are invited to play, reflect, and build, they stop being “participants” and start being “architects.”
